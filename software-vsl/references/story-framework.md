# Demo-led VSL story framework

This framework combines direct-response functions commonly associated with Ryan Deiss's VSL teaching with patterns observed in software launch videos. It is a practical adaptation, not a claim about an exact or proprietary canonical formula.

## Full front-end VSL

Select and order the functions that the offer needs:

1. **Hook:** name or show the transformation quickly.
2. **Problem and promise:** identify the buyer's situation and what the video will resolve.
3. **Reason to keep watching:** promise a meaningful demonstration or answer, not “something exciting.”
4. **Friction and agitation:** show the time, cost, uncertainty, or repeated work in the current approach.
5. **Mechanism reveal:** explain why this product changes the process.
6. **Demo:** real input, essential intermediate state, user control, and finished output.
7. **Desired next step:** connect the output to the buyer's job or result.
8. **Proof and credentials:** use verifiable output, relevant maker history, or correctly attributed customer evidence.
9. **Offer, bonuses, and value:** exact product, access, allowances, separation from optional items, price, and payment shape. Give each bonus a distinct job. Frame value or price against a supported, relevant alternative rather than an invented total.
10. **Guarantee:** plain-language policy matching the real terms.
11. **Proportional boundary:** when it resolves a real objection, state what the tool does not automate or what the buyer still chooses. It does not need a dedicated beat when a short phrase elsewhere is enough.
12. **Action and urgency:** one clear next step. State a deadline, changing price, or limited availability only when it is genuine and consistent with checkout behavior.

Classic direct response often delays the solution until after agitation. Software launch VSLs can front-load a strong demo because the visible transformation itself earns attention and answers skepticism. Choose based on the offer's strongest evidence.

## OTO adaptation

A useful short upsell sequence is:

1. Purchase acknowledgement without a long celebration.
2. The next bottleneck created or revealed by using the main product.
3. A brief reason to watch.
4. Concrete friction.
5. Add-on reveal.
6. Two or three contiguous demo steps.
7. The resulting workflow or desired state.
8. Evidence or included resources.
9. Guarantee.
10. Exact incremental offer.
11. Honest boundary and compatibility with the base purchase.
12. Accept or continue.

Never imply that the buyer accepted a prior optional upsell. A downsell can be much shorter: explain the reduced capacity or scope, retain the core mechanism, and state the new price without replaying the full problem story.

## Script audit

- Read every spoken line without visuals. Does each beat add meaning?
- Read only on-screen lines. Do they form a coherent argument and match the narration?
- Resolve vague referents: “this,” “it,” “another,” “one more part,” and “what comes next.” Preserve natural fragments and questions when their meaning is clear.
- Confirm the demo proves the exact claim. Generation-stage timing does not prove end-to-end timing.
- Separate product behavior from buyer action: drafting outreach is not sending it; locating an available email is not guaranteeing an owner reply.
- Keep technical charging rules concise in narration. Put edge cases on the page or in FAQ unless they are central to the buying decision.
- Match the guarantee exactly. An encouraged usage challenge is not automatically a refund condition.
- Remove stale prices, dates, credit counts, screenshots, captions, and verbal references together.
